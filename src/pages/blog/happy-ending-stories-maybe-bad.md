When I was a kid, one of my favorite shows was [Doraemon](https://doraemon.fandom.com/wiki/Doraemon). I watched it every Sunday morning—I believe it was from 8am until 8.30am. I also had the privilege to read the weekly Donald Duck magazine. Additionally, who can forget about Captain Tsubasa and Yu-Gi-Oh shows? I enjoyed them all when I was still a kid, but I only enjoy some of them now that I have tasted the spice of life.

In this post, I will use the [age rating from Parental Guidelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_Parental_Guidelines#Ratings). For example: TV-G means suitable for all ages and TV-Y7 means suitable for age 7 and above.

Every kid/preteen story (with TV-Y, TV-Y7, TV-G rating, or even higher ones such as TV-PG) usually revolves around 2 themes: hero and discrete (non-continuation). Both themes tend to end with happy endings and aren't preparing them for reality. Or maybe our community just sucks? Or maybe I just say this because my perspective comes with age? Well, all three can be true at once. Let's dissect each of the themes.

## Story themes

### Hero

A story for kids/preteens usually has main character(s). It's not a secret either that the main character _always_ wins regardless of situation. If their back is against the wall, they will come back and win. It's just a matter of the twists and turns so the story can "drag" longer. Most importantly, they have plot armor. They can be injured, but they never face consequences. Let's get some examples.

A hero's story usually consists of a series of episodes. After all, they are the main character and they need to go through a journey so that they have a "payoff". In [Captain Tsubasa](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt7784442/parentalguide/#certificates) (rated TV-G in the US or age 6-15 in other countries), the boy was very talented in football. He carried his school's team to victory every single time, whether he was fit or injured. It's crazy! No way in the real world you are allowed to play a football match with a broken... shoulder? Well... maybe there are some, like [Declan Rice when being asked to play for England in the World Cup](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/7438250/2026/07/13/arsenal-injuries-rice-odegaard-saka/)... but it's very rare. Most of the time they would never make it to the bench. Besides, if they force themselves to play while carrying an injury, they will risk getting a worse one, which eventually means getting sidelined for months.

Another example is [Yu-Gi-Oh](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0249327/parentalguide/) (rated TV-PG on Netflix and age 7-15 in other countries), although I'd say the writers were _a bit more nuanced_ when they wrote the plot. Yugi/Atem almost never lost a game, barring five matches as explained in [this YouTube short](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/wjdkdHUwK7k). Every other game, there were twists and turns but eventually, Yugi/Atem came out on top. From the previous YouTube shorts link, I consider the only loss to be the one vs. Rafael. Yes, the English-dubbed one that gave birth to the legendary meme: _"It should have been me, not him!"_

A bit of context on that one, so it was a "filler arc" (some might say) where there were groups of people who wanted to feed a giant snake "Leviathan". They do so by winning a duel against another person when a field card called "Seal of Orichalcos" is active. When the opponent loses the game, their soul is removed from their body and "locked" in a temple. Yugi/Atem battled against Rafael and Rafael tempted Atem to use Seal of Orichalcos because otherwise, he would lose the match. He eventually succumbed and used it, which gave him a lead in the match, but Rafael turned it around and won the match. Since Seal of Orichalcos was active, Atem's soul was supposed to be captured, but instead, Yugi pushed him out and paid the price.

That was _probably_ the only one time that I can recall where a conscious action in Yu-Gi-Oh led to a proper consequence. Atem even went on a bit of a soul-searching journey to forgive himself. I think that part of the story was beautiful.

All that is to say, I think, consuming too many hero-themed stories (without proper nuance) will make a kid seemingly... eager to become a "hero". A stalwart of justice. A guiding light. However, we adults know that real life doesn't work that way, so why should we shape kids/preteens' mental model with the hero mental model?

### Discrete

Discrete is the opposite of continuation. Both of the examples in the previous section, Captain Tsubasa and Yu-Gi-Oh, are "continued" stories. They have seasons, they have episodes. What happened in the prior episodes will be used as a foundation for future episodes, and so on, and so forth. Discrete stories, on the other hand, tell the story as if the characters are on a closed time-loop. How?

Take [Doraemon](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0069576/parentalguide/) as an example (rated TV-PG in the US and up to 13 years old in other countries). Every comic or show episode is built on the fact that the story _always_ takes place in a snapshot of time. Summer, winter, spring, fall, but it's always when Nobita and friends were still in elementary school. Even the adventure comics/movies were based on the same snapshot. Yes, they could travel to the future using the time machine, but it's non-consequential.

Whatever Nobita did, it was "isolated" in that episode and that episode alone. It couldn't "leak" into other episodes. Hence, no consequences. Nobita asked Doraemon to pull out a [Tax Bird](https://doraemon.fandom.com/wiki/Tax_Bird_(Gadget))? People were only taxed in that episode alone. In other episodes, that gadget doesn't exist nor is it ever mentioned. Hence, the consequence is just simply a whiplash-like sensation. One second you get a consequence, the following one it's no longer a consequence.

Same thing with Donald Duck (I can't find it but it _does_ seem like it's rated TV-G). Regardless of the main character in the episode (it can vary between Donald/Scrooge/Mickey/Goofy/Huey-Dewey-Louie/Gyro/others), whatever actions they do in that episode, they only happen in that episode. Scrooge's money bin got demolished by the Beagle Boys? The next episode, it's back to normal. Yes, again, there may be consequences in the episode, but if they only happen in a short time and are never mentioned again in the future, who cares?

## My thoughts

Now, there is a reason why I put the "maybe" in parentheses in the post title, because I am not entirely sure of it. My current thought is that we should limit stories with TV-Y or TV-G rating and adjust the content age rating accordingly as a child grows.

This is because stories with TV-Y or TV-G rating are pretty much hero-themed stories or discrete stories with few learnings to extract. Even the examples above, such as Captain Tsubasa and Yu-Gi-Oh, have TV-PG ratings and yet, they are full of "main character energy". Hence, consuming shows/comics with TV-Y or TV-G rating too much can give them a "skewed" sense of reality. From a "realm" where the main character always wins and few conflicts into real life with a lot of injustice and conflicts.

I don't think there is any form of discrimination in any story with TV-Y or TV-G rating, but in real life, you may see a lot, be it discrimination by race, skin color, or wealth. In Indonesia, speaking from experience, this can start as early as elementary school (age 6-12). Of course, it heavily depends on the community. If the community is built on and familiar with diversity, then I doubt any differences will matter at all.

On the other hand, I don't deny that we shouldn't deny kids/preteens their imagination. Maybe they dream of a world akin to what they see in their favorite shows or comics? Then, let them be. Preemptively restricting their imagination may do more harm than good, at least [based on this article](https://bimontlab.com/why-children-are-imagining-less-and-what-we-can-do-about-it/):

> Experts agree that children do not need more instructions. They need more freedom. Instead of teaching creativity like a step-by-step guide, adults should act as facilitators. The goal is not to provide the answer, but to guide exploration.

So... maybe, what parents can do is that, if their kids are to consume content with age rating TV-G or TV-Y, let them do that until 7 years old. After that, parents should encourage more TV-Y7 content (where appropriate). That way, they are getting acclimated early to a "darker world" and won't have a huge "culture shock" from their sunshine-and-rainbow fantasy. By engaging with heavier stories that their children consume, parents may have a better bond with children while also giving insights and perspective to their children.

It's funny, I recall playing Red Alert 2 and Warcraft III campaigns during elementary school and all that I cared about was the gameplay. I didn't really care about the story. I wonder what my response would be if the kid version of me was asked, _"What do you think about Arthas? Was he right or wrong? Why?"_ I think those kinds of questions really help shape nuances that otherwise don't really exist in stories with TV-Y or TV-G rating.

## Closing words

So, yeah, that's all I have to share. I am not entirely sure if this is useful, but I hope this can be thought-provoking. I believe it is better to familiarize kids with the dark side of the world via stories rather than having the kids experience it themselves for the first time. For example, how often we see bullying being unpunished in those content? Never. The bullies are always punished. In real life, justice is not guaranteed, so parents may have to "set expectations" to their children. I know, I know, we probably don't need to do that if _all_ parents do their job well in raising their kids so their kids won't bully other kids (physically or mentally).

I also don't like the fact that sometimes, when kids share with their parents that they were being bullied at school, their parents would recommend training martial arts instead to "fight back". Why should the victim be the one who "does the work"? Why can't it be the perpetrator who does so? It's absurd—one doesn't need to reach adolescence to see impunity.
