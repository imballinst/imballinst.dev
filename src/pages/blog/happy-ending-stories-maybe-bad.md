When I was a kid, one of my favorite shows was [Doraemon](https://doraemon.fandom.com/wiki/Doraemon). I watched it every Sunday morning, I believe it was from 8am until 8.30am. I also had the privilege to read the weekly Donald Duck magazine. Additionally, who can forget about Captain Tsubasa and Yu-Gi-Oh shows? I enjoyed them all when I was still a kid, but I only enjoy some of them now that I have tasted the spice of life.

Every kid/preteen story (with General Audience age rating) usually revolves around 2 themes: hero and discrete (non-continuation). Both of which, I think, aren't preparing them for reality. Or maybe our community just sucks? Or maybe I just say this because perspective comes with age? Well, all three can be true at once. Let's disect each of the themes.

## Story themes

### Hero

A story for kids/preteens usually have main character(s). It's not a secret either that the main character _always_ wins regardless of situation. If their back is against the wall, they will come back and win. It's just the matter of the twists and turns so the story can "drag" longer. Most importantly, they have plot armor. They can be injured, but they never face consequences. Let's get some examples.

A hero's story usually consists in a series of episodes. After all, they are the main character and they need to go through a journey so that they have a "payoff". In Captain Tsubasa, the boy was very talented in football. He carried his school's team to victory every single time, whether he's fit or injured. It's crazy! No way in real world you are allowed to play a football match with a broken... shoulder? Well... maybe there are some, like [Declan Rice when being asked to play for England in the World Cup](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/7438250/2026/07/13/arsenal-injuries-rice-odegaard-saka/)... but it's very rare. Most of the times they would never make it to the bench. Besides, if they force themselves to play while carrying an injury, they will risk themselves getting a worse one, which eventually means getting sidelined for months.

Another example is Yu-Gi-Oh, although I'd say the writers were more _a bit more nuanced_ when they wrote the plot. Yugi/Atem almost never lost a game, barring five matches as explained in [this YouTube shorts](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/wjdkdHUwK7k). Every other game, there were twists and turns but eventually, Yugi/Atem came out on top. From the previous YouTube shorts link, I only _consider_ the only loss being the one vs. Rafael. Yes, the English-dubbed one that gave birth to the legendary meme: _"It should have been me, not him!"_

A bit on context on that one, so it was a "filler arc" (some might say) where there were groups of people who wanted to feed a giant snake "Leviathan". They do so by winning a duel against another person when a field card called "Seal of Orichalcos" is active. When the opponent loses the game, their soul is removed from their body and "locked" in a temple. Yugi/Atem battled against Rafael and Rafael tempted Atem to use Seal of Orichalcos because otherwise, he would lose the match. He eventually succumbed and used it, which gave him a lead in the match, but Rafael turned it around and won the match. Since Seal of Orichalcos was active, Atem's soul was supposed to be captured, but instead, Yugi pushed him out and took the price instead.

That was _probably_ the only one time that I can recall where a conscious action in Yu-Gi-Oh that led to a proper consequence. Atem even went on a bit of soul searching journey to forgive himself. I think that part of the story was beautiful.

All that is to say, I think, too many consuming hero-themed stories (without proper nuance) will make a kid seemingly... eager to become a "hero". A stalwart of justice. A guiding light. However, we adults know that real life doesn't work that way, so why should we shape kids/preteens' mental model with the hero mental model?

### Discrete

Discrete is the opposite of continuation. Both of the examples in the previous section, Captain Tsubasa and Yu-Gi-Oh, are a "continued" story. They have seasons, they have episodes. What happened in the prior episodes will be used as a foundation for future episodes, and so on, and so forth. Discrete stories, on the other hand, tell the story as if the characters are on a closed time-loop. How?

Take Doraemon as an example. Every comic or show episode is built on the fact that the story _always_ takes place in a snapshot of time. Summer, winter, spring, fall, but it's always when Nobita and friends were still in elementary school. Even the adventure comics/movies were also based on the same snapshot. Yes, they could travel to the future using the time machine, but it's non-consequential.

Whatever thing that Nobita did, it was "isolated" in that episode and that episode alone. It couldn't "leak" into other episodes. Hence, no consequences. Nobita asked Doraemon to pull out a [Tax Bird](https://doraemon.fandom.com/wiki/Tax_Bird_(Gadget))? People were only taxed in that episode alone. In other episodes, that gadget doesn't exist nor is it ever mentioned. Hence, the consequence is just simply a whiplash-like sensation. One second you gets a consequencce, the following one it's no longer a consequence.

Same thing with Donald Duck. Regardless of the main character in the episode (it can be varying between Donald/Scrooge/Mickey/Goofy/Huey-Dewey-Louie/Gyro/others), whatever actions they do in that episode, it only happens in that episode. Scrooge's money bin got demolished by the Beagle Boys? The next episode, it's back to normal. Yes, again, there may be consequences in the episode, but if it's only happening in a short time and is never mentioned again in the future, who cares?

## My thoughts

Now, there is a reason why I put the "maybe" in a parentheses in the post title, because I am not entirely sure of it. My current thought is that, we should limit stories with General Audience age rating and make those with Parental Guidance age rating the popular ones. At least, for kids/preteens who are already going to school. Things with General Audience age rating don't need parental guidance, so it's safe for kids/preteens to "consume" them alone.

However, consuming shows/comics with General Audience rating too much can give them a "skewed" sense of reality. From a "realm" where the main character always wins and little conflicts into real life with a lot of injustice and conflicts. I don't think there is any form of discriminiation in any story with General Audience age rating, but in real life, you may see a lot, be it discrimination by race, skin color, or wealth. In Indonesia, speaking from experience, this can start as soon as from elementary school. Of course, it heavily depends on the community. If the community is built on and familiar with diversity, then I doubt any differences will matter at all.

On the other hand, I don't deny that we shouldn't deny kids/preteens from their imagination. Maybe they dream of a world akin to what they see in their favorite shows or comics? Then, let them be. Preemptively restricting their imagination may do more harm than good, at least [based on this article](https://bimontlab.com/why-children-are-imagining-less-and-what-we-can-do-about-it/):

> Experts agree that children do not need more instructions. They need more freedom. Instead of teaching creativity like a step-by-step guide, adults should act as facilitators. The goal is not to provide the answer, but to guide exploration.

So... maybe, General Audience until 7 and after that, encourage more Parental Guidance content (where appropriate [depending on age](https://pegi.info/what-do-the-labels-mean))? That way, they are getting acclimated early about the real world and won't have a huge "culture shock" from their fantasy. Probably parents have a big role on this, because what's a "Parental Guidance" without a parent role? It's just the same playing a story-based game for the gameplay alone.

It's funny, I recall myself playing Red Alert 2 and Warcraft III campaigns during elementary school and all that I cared was the gameplay. I didn't really care about the story. I wonder what would be my response if the kid version of me was asked, _"What do you think about Arthas? Was he right or wrong? Why?"_ I think those kind of questions really help shape nuances that are otherwise don't really exist in stories with General Audience age rating.
